Suicide Pass 2 (SUI PASS 2)

How I think about the nil, the cover, and the two-card pass — with a few hands to make it concrete.

Quick tips Why the cover Nil or bid Passing as nil Passing as cover Add two

QUICK TIPS

  1. Decide early whether you're setting the nil or the cover. With two hands protecting it, the nil is hard to break, and at only 50 points, setting the cover is usually the better play.
  2. A strong hand is often best for nil, because the main idea is to give your partner a strong hand.
  3. When you cannot give partner that help, it is often best to bid — be the cover, and avoid setting them.
  4. As the nil hand, always pass to strengthen the cover, unless the alternative is extreme peril — a lone King, for example.
  5. As cover, pass chiefly to strengthen your own hand, within reason. A good option, often, is to pass to make yourself short-suited.
  6. Always add two to the bid, trusting your partner to pass two winners.

Why the cover hand matters

Pick your target: the nil or the cover

Trying to set a nil means playing different cards than if you're trying to set the cover, so you need to decide which is your goal. With two hands covering nils, a nil is not easy to break, and you risk setting yourself trying to set it. Additionally, a nil in SUI PASS 2 is only worth 50 points, so setting the cover will be a push even if your side's nil is broken.

Deciding whether to nil

The main idea is to give your partner a strong hand

A strong hand is often best for the nil. That may sound backwards, but it isn't. If you have the strength, you can pass it to your partner and leave them a real cover hand. That is the point of the pass.

When you cannot — the hand is too weak to help them, or too dangerous to nil — it is often best to bid. Be the cover. You avoid putting your partner on a nil you cannot support, and you avoid setting them.

Example · A strong hand

You pick up:

A♠ K♠ 9♠
A♥ J♥ 6♥
K♦ 8♦ 4♦
9♣ 7♣ 2♣

Gold outline marks a natural pass to partner — two winners.

This is a strong hand. Nil is often best, because you can pass two winners — the ♠A and ♠K, or the ♠A and ♥A — and give your partner a strong hand. That is the main idea.

Example · When you cannot

You pick up:

Q♠ 8♠ 5♠ 2♠
K♥ 10♥ 7♥
J♦ 9♦ 6♦
Q♣ 8♣ 3♣

No clean winners to give, and plenty of ways to get caught if you nil. You cannot give your partner a strong hand. Bid, and be the cover, so you are not setting them.

Passing as the nil hand

Strengthen the cover — unless the alternative is extreme peril

As the nil hand, you always pass to strengthen the cover hand. Give them winners. That is what the pass is for.

The exception is extreme peril. A lone King, for example — if keeping it is likely to catch you, pass it off, even if that means the cover gets a slightly less helpful card.

Example · Strengthen the cover

You have bid nil. You hold:

A♠ Q♠ 6♠
K♥ 8♥ 4♥
10♦ 7♦ 3♦
9♣ 5♣ 2♣
Pass ♠A and ♥K

Send the two winners. Keep the rest and play the nil. Your job now is to stay under; their job is to take the tricks — so give them the cards that do that.

Example · Extreme peril — a lone King

You have bid nil. You hold:

A♠ 7♠ 4♠
K♥
Q♦ 9♦ 8♦ 3♦
10♣ 6♣ 5♣ 2♣
Pass ♥K and ♠A

The ♥K is a lone King. If you keep it, it is extreme peril. Pass it off — and you can still send a winner, the ♠A, along with it. Do not ship two side winners and leave that bare King sitting in your hand.

Passing as cover

Strengthen your own hand, within reason

As cover, you also pass chiefly to strengthen your own hand, within reason. You are the one who has to take the tricks, so the pass should make that job easier — not just shuffle the deck around.

A good option, often, is to pass to make yourself short-suited. Void a side suit, or get down to one card in it, and those trumps start working for you.

Example · Short-suit yourself

You are the cover. You hold:

K♠ Q♠ 10♠ 8♠
A♥ 9♥ 6♥ 3♥
A♦ 7♦ 4♦
8♣ 3♣
Pass ♣8 and ♣3

Pass both clubs and make yourself void. Now you can ruff clubs, and your own hand is stronger for it. That is often a better pass than poking at a hand that is already fairly solid.

Within reason. Short-suiting is a good option — not a command. Don't gut the hand just to void a suit if it costs you a card you need.

Always add two

Trust your partner to pass two winners

You should always add two to the bid, trusting your partner to pass two winners. If your hand looks like a 4, bid 6. Those two tricks are on the way — count them before you have even seen them.

That trust runs both ways. The nil hand is supposed to send you help. Bid as if they will.

A simple habit. Count your own tricks, then add two. Don't shade it back to “maybe one.” The whole variation is built on that pass.
