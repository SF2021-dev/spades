The common mistake

Sets get the attention. Bags do the damage.

In my opinion, a common mistake is too much effort trying to set opponents and too little attention to bags.

In CUT, every player is for themselves. There is no partner to soak up the extra tricks. The game is to 400. With only 400 to win, a bag penalty or a set swings a game fast. If your habit is to hunt a set on every hand, you will take bags you did not need, and sometimes you will set yourself in the bargain. The better habit is to take care of your own bid and your own bags first, and only go after a set when it is really there.

Why chasing sets costs you

You pay for the attempt, whether it works or not

Trying to set an opponent usually means taking tricks you do not need for your own bid. Those overtricks are bags, and bags add up to a penalty. A set that costs you a handful of bags is often not the bargain it felt like at the table.

And if the set fails, you have still spent the cards. You may have used the winners you needed to make your own bid, and now you are the one who is set. You paid, and you got nothing for it.

Example

You bid 4, and you already have your four. An opponent still needs a trick. You have winners left, so you cash them, hoping to shut that player out. They make anyway. Those extra tricks are bags you took for a set that never happened. If, instead, you throw those winners early and then come up short of your 4, you have set yourself trying to set someone else.

Managing bags

Treat bags as part of the score, not an afterthought

Bags add up to a penalty. That is easy to forget in the middle of a hand, when setting someone feels like the prize. Keep an eye on your bag count the way you keep an eye on the bids.

Once your bid is in, stop taking tricks you do not need. A "free" overtrick is still a bag. If you can duck, throw off, or otherwise stay on your number, do that — unless you have a clear, cheap set sitting in front of you.

A simple habit. After you make your bid, ask yourself whether the next trick is for a real set, or just another bag.

When a set is worth going for

Go after it when it's clearly there — not as a habit

I am not saying never set anyone. A set is worth going for when you can do it with the tricks you were going to take anyway, or when the opponent is clearly short and you will not bury yourself in bags to finish them off. A nil is worth 100, so that is a real swing — but only if you can take it without a pile of bags.

If the set needs you to cash extra winners, or to empty your hand of the cards that protect your own bid, it is probably too expensive. Let them make, stay on your number, and keep the bags down.

Bidding to avoid overtricks

Bid a number you can make cleanly

A lot of bag trouble starts in the bid. If you bid low and then take a pile of overtricks, those are bags. If you bid more than you can make, you will be scrambling, and that is when you start making desperate plays.

Bid a number you can actually make, without counting on a lucky leftover or two, and without stretching so thin that one missed trick sets you. Then play to that number. The sets you take will be the ones that fall into your lap, not the ones you chased.
